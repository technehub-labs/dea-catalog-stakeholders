## Definition

The US Securities and Exchange Commission is the federal agency that protects investors, maintains fair, orderly, and efficient markets, and facilitates capital formation. It enforces the federal securities laws: rulemaking, registration and disclosure oversight (EDGAR), examinations of regulated entities, and civil enforcement — including the anti-insider-trading regime this catalog's sibling record anchors.

The record's normative description (verbatim): *The United States Securities and Exchange Commission, the federal regulator that enforces US securities law, including the insider-trading prohibitions anchored in the regulations catalog.*

## Mandate and goals

- Protect investors (the SEC's first statutory mission).
- Maintain fair, orderly, and efficient markets.
- Facilitate capital formation.
- Enforce the securities laws through examinations and civil enforcement actions.

## What they do

- Adopts and maintains securities-law rules (including the insider-trading regime's disclosure obligations).
- Operates EDGAR, the public disclosure system for regulated filings.
- Examines regulated entities (registrants, advisers, broker-dealers) for compliance.
- Brings civil enforcement actions, and operates a whistleblower program that rewards high-quality tips.

## Engagement with the enterprise

The enterprise's financial-stewardship and investment activities are conducted to remain compliant with the regime this authority enforces; the enforced-regime record anchors the relationship. Should the enterprise ever hold registrant status, the SEC's disclosure and insider-trading regimes would bind directly.
